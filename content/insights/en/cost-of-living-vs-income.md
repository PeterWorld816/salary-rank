## A percentile is a ranking, not a report card

The most important thing to understand about any income percentile — the
ones on this site included — is what it deliberately leaves out. It ranks
your income against other people's income. It does not know what your rent
is, what your state takes in tax, or what a gallon of milk costs where you
live.

That gap between "ranks well" and "feels well-off" is where most of the
confusion about income statistics lives. Two households with identical
incomes, one in Mississippi and one outside Washington, D.C., will get very
similar national percentiles and lead very different financial lives.

## The clearest example: $200,000 in two places

Take a household earning $200,000 a year. Nationally, that's a strong
position: **13.4% of US households** report $200,000 or more, so this
household sits comfortably inside the top 15% of the country.

Now put that household in Loudoun County, Virginia, in the D.C. suburbs.
Loudoun's median household income is **$181,765**, and **44.7% of households
there earn at least $200,000.**

The same $200,000 household is:

- **Top 13.4% nationally**
- Barely above the **local median** in Loudoun County

Nothing about their finances changed. Their national ranking says they're
doing better than roughly six households in seven. Their local ranking says
they're an ordinary household on their own street — and it's the local
figure that determines what they'll pay for a house, what their neighbors'
lifestyles look like, and what "normal" feels like day to day.

Run the same exercise in the other direction and it's just as stark. In
Mississippi, only **5.8%** of households reach $200,000. That same income is
top-6% territory there. In D.C., **26.7%** of households clear it — more
than one in four. See [the full state
ranking](/us/insights/state-median-income-rankings) for how wide that spread
runs across all fifty states.

## Where the money actually goes

Three things separate a high income from a high standard of living, and none
of them appear in a percentile.

**Housing.** This is the big one. Housing is the largest line item in most
household budgets, and it varies between metro areas far more than incomes
do. A local labor market that pays 40% above the national median can easily
sit in a housing market that costs multiples of the national norm. When
housing eats a disproportionate share of gross income, everything downstream
— saving, childcare, retirement contributions — competes for a much smaller
remainder. Two households at the same income with very different housing
burdens are not in the same financial position, and no income statistic will
tell you which is which.

**State and local taxes.** Every figure on this site, and in the Census
tables behind it, is *pre-tax* income. But the US doesn't tax income
uniformly by geography. A handful of states — Texas, Florida, Washington,
and Nevada among them — levy no personal income tax at all, while others
apply progressive state rates on top of federal ones. Local income taxes
exist in some cities and not others. Two households with identical gross
income and identical percentiles can end up with materially different
take-home pay purely because of where they filed. States without an income
tax typically raise revenue elsewhere, through property or sales taxes, so
the comparison is rarely as simple as "no income tax means more money" — but
it's never nothing, either.

**Everything else that's priced locally.** Childcare, insurance,
transportation, utilities. These don't move in lockstep with local wages,
and they're not captured anywhere in a household income figure.

## Why the local percentile is usually the more useful number

Given all that, the national percentile is the *less* informative of the two
numbers this site gives you.

Your local percentile — county, and where available, town — is measured
against households facing broadly the same housing market, the same state
tax code, and the same local prices as you. It won't correct for cost of
living, because it isn't a cost-of-living adjustment. But it does compare
you to people whose costs resemble yours, which is a rough and useful proxy
for the same thing.

This is exactly why the site drills down from national to state to county to
town rather than stopping at one number. The [Loudoun County
page](/us/va/51107), for instance, shows what a national top-15% income
looks like against local reality — and every one of the 3,000-plus counties
has the same breakdown.

## How to read your own result honestly

A few rules that keep percentiles useful rather than misleading:

**Read both numbers, not one.** If your national and local percentiles are
far apart, that gap is itself the finding. A wide gap in the "nationally
higher" direction means you live somewhere with a comparatively low local
income base; the opposite means you're in a high-cost, high-income area
where your ranking is diluted by well-paid neighbors.

**Don't treat a strong percentile as proof you're fine.** Ranking in the top
20% while spending most of your income on housing is a real and common
situation. The percentile is describing your position in a distribution, not
auditing your budget.

**Don't treat a weak percentile as proof you're behind.** A mid-range
national ranking in a low-cost county can support a more comfortable life
than a strong ranking in an expensive metro. If you're weighing a move, the
question worth asking isn't "what percentile does this salary put me in" but
"what does this salary buy in that specific place" — and the local median on
each county page is a far better starting point for that than any national
figure.

**Remember these are pre-tax figures.** Every number here, everywhere on
this site, is gross household income as the Census collects it.
