## The misunderstanding that skews every result

Someone types $60,000 into an income calculator, sees "top 62%," and
concludes they're behind. Someone else types $60,000, sees the same number,
and concludes they're doing fine because their spouse earns another $60,000.

Only one of them read the result correctly — and it's the second one, but
only if they entered $120,000 instead.

Nearly every headline US income statistic, including the **$80,734** median
this site is built on, measures *household* income: everyone living under
one roof, combined, from every source — wages, self-employment, investment
income, retirement income, the lot. It is not one person's salary. The
Census Bureau's table B19013 counts households, not workers, and the bracket
table underneath it (B19001) does the same.

That distinction changes the answer more than almost anything else you could
get wrong.

## The same salary, two very different percentiles

Take the national anchor points directly. Where a household lands:

| Household income | National percentile |
|---|---|
| $50,000 | top 68.6% |
| $100,000 | top 40.5% |

Now consider two people who both personally earn $50,000 a year.

The first lives alone. Their household income is $50,000, and they sit
around the top 69% nationally — meaning roughly 69% of US households report
more.

The second is married to someone who also earns $50,000. Their household
income is $100,000, and that household sits around the top 41%.

**Same personal salary. A gap of roughly 28 percentage points.** Neither
person got a raise; one of them just has a second earner in the house. If
you enter only your own paycheck while living in a dual-income household,
you're comparing your half against everyone else's whole — and the result
will look worse than your actual situation.

## Why one-person and four-person households aren't comparable

The household framing creates a second trap running the other direction.

A $95,000 single-person household and a $95,000 household of four land on
exactly the same percentile. The statistics say they're identical. Anyone
who has paid for childcare knows they aren't.

Household income counts money coming in. It says nothing about how many
people that money has to support, and the Census doesn't adjust for
household size in these tables. So the percentile is a genuine ranking of
income, not a ranking of financial comfort.

You can see the effect in the Census's own married/single breakdown. In
Ohio, median household income is $71,389 overall — but **$111,943** for
married-couple households and **$42,800** for single-person households. In
Texas it's $115,089 versus $48,857. Those aren't two groups of people with
different earning power so much as two groups with different numbers of
earners. Every state page on this site shows this split for that state,
which is why comparing yourself to the *right* household type usually moves
your result more than you'd expect.

## Why this site uses household income anyway

Given all that, it's fair to ask why we don't just use individual salaries.

The short answer: household income is what the Census publishes at the
geographic detail this site needs. B19001 gives income brackets for the
nation, every state, and all 3,000-plus counties — which is what makes a
county-level percentile possible at all. There is no equivalent
individual-earnings bracket table published down to the county level.

The longer answer is that household income is usually the more meaningful
unit anyway. Rent, groceries, and childcare are paid out of a household
budget, not out of one person's paycheck. If you want to know how your
economic position compares to your neighbors', the combined figure is
closer to the truth than either salary alone.

Every percentile here traces back to a published Census table — see [what an
income percentile actually
means](/us/insights/understanding-income-percentiles) for how the
interpolation works.

## How to read your result if you only want to compare your own salary

You have two reasonable options, and they answer different questions.

**Option 1 — enter your household total.** Add up everything everyone in
your home earns. The percentile you get answers: "How does my household's
economic position compare to other households in my county, state, and the
country?" This is the comparison the data was designed for, and the one to
use if you're asking whether you're doing well by local standards.

**Option 2 — enter your salary alone, and reinterpret the label.** If you
enter $60,000 as a single earner, what you're really asking is: "How would a
$60,000 household rank?" That's a legitimate question, but be clear that
you're benchmarking against *households*, many of which contain two earners.
Your result will systematically understate where you personally stand as a
worker.

For a cleaner individual comparison, the state pages carry the Census's
median individual earnings figures alongside the household numbers — in
Ohio, for instance, median earnings are $51,435 for men and $37,051 for
women, both well below that state's $71,389 household median. That gap
between the household line and the individual lines *is* the second-earner
effect, made visible.

## The one-sentence version

If you live with someone who earns, enter both incomes; if you enter only
yours, read the answer as "where a household earning this much would rank,"
not "where I rank as a worker."
