## The full picture: cost of living, geography, and industry mix

Median household income varies by nearly **$53,000** between the
highest-ranked and lowest-ranked state — a gap driven by cost of living,
industry mix, and geography, not just "who works harder." A $70,000 income
in rural Mississippi and a $70,000 income in the DC suburbs buy very
different lives.

All figures below are median household income from the Census Bureau's ACS
2020–2024 5-Year Estimates (table B19013) — the same data every state page
on this site uses.

A word on what "median household income" means before the tables, because
it changes how you should read them. These are *household* figures:
everyone in the home, combined, from every source of income — not one
person's salary. States with more dual-earner households will rank higher
partly for that reason alone, independent of what any individual job pays
there. The national median across every household is $80,734.

Thirteen states are separated by about $6,100 here — roughly the same as the

the top and bottom handful, a state's rank is close to meaningless as a

Notice too that most of this middle band sits *below* the national median of
$80,734. That isn't a contradiction — the national figure is a median over
households, not over states, and the most populous high-income states pull
it upward. See [what's the average US

## A state median hides more than it shows

The single biggest limitation of any state ranking is that states are not
economically uniform. Virginia's median household income is $93,170, which
puts it comfortably in the upper half of the table. But Loudoun County,
inside that same state, has a median of **$181,765** — nearly double the
state figure, and higher than any state's median on this page.

That pattern repeats everywhere. A state's number is an average over
metropolitan counties, suburban counties, and rural ones with completely
different labour markets. Ranking 15th or 40th tells you very little about
what a specific county looks like, which is why every state page here drills
down county by county rather than stopping at the state line.

The same is true at the top of the distribution. The share of households
earning $200,000 or more ranges from 5.8% in Mississippi to 26.7% in D.C. —
a spread far wider than the medians alone suggest. [What income puts you in
the top 1%](/us/insights/top-1-percent-income-threshold) breaks that down
state by state.

## What this ranking doesn't tell you

A high rank doesn't automatically mean "better off" — it doesn't account for
state or local taxes, or how far that income actually goes locally. A
household earning the median in a high-ranked state can still feel squeezed
once rent and childcare are priced in.

That caveat deserves more weight than it usually gets. Housing costs, state
income tax rules, and childcare prices all vary by geography in ways that
income rankings simply don't capture — a top-10 state can be a harder place
to get ahead than a bottom-10 one, depending entirely on what you spend.
[Why a high income doesn't always mean you're
ahead](/us/insights/cost-of-living-vs-income) walks through how far apart
those two things can drift.
