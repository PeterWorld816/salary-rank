## The actual number

The median US household income is **$80,734**, based on the Census Bureau's
American Community Survey (ACS) 2020–2024 5-Year Estimates (table B19013) —
the same figure this whole site is built on.

That's the number most people are actually looking for when they search
"average US salary." It isn't, technically, an average, and it isn't a
salary. Both of those distinctions matter more than they sound.

## "Median" vs. "average" aren't the same thing

- **Median** is the middle value — half of US households earn more than
  $80,734, half earn less.
- **Average (mean)** adds up every household's income and divides by the
  number of households. A relatively small number of very high earners pulls
  the mean well above the median.

Income distributions are lopsided — a long tail of high earners, not a
symmetric bell curve — so the mean and median tell different stories. The
median is the more representative "what does a typical household make"
number, which is why the Census Bureau leads with it, and why this site uses
it too.

## Where $80,734 actually sits in the distribution

A single midpoint doesn't tell you much about the shape around it. These are
the real bracket boundaries the Census publishes in table B19001 — the
anchor points every percentile on this site is interpolated between:

| Household income | Share of US households at or above it |
|---|---|
| $200,000 | 13.4% |
| $150,000 | 23.0% |
| $125,000 | 30.4% |
| $100,000 | 40.5% |
| $75,000 | 53.1% |
| $50,000 | 68.6% |
| $25,000 | 85.4% |

Two things in that table tend to surprise people.

First, a six-figure household income is not unusual: **40.5%** of US
households — two in five — report $100,000 or more. It's above the median,
but nowhere near rare.

Second, look at how much ground the bottom of the table covers. The gap
between $25,000 and $50,000 moves you nearly 17 percentage points, while the
gap between $150,000 and $200,000 moves you less than 10. Households are
packed densely in the middle and thin out fast at both ends, which is
exactly why the mean sits so far above the median.

Notice also where the table stops. The highest bracket the Census publishes
is "$200,000 or more" — open-ended — so the data can rank you precisely
through the middle of the distribution but goes blurry at the very top. That
limitation is the whole subject of [what income puts you in the top
1%](/us/insights/top-1-percent-income-threshold).

## "Household income," not "individual salary"

$80,734 is *household* income — everyone living in the home, combined, from
every source (wages, self-employment, investment income, etc.), not one
person's paycheck. A married couple who each earn $45,000 shows up in this
data as one $90,000 household, not two $45,000 salaries.

This is the single most common way people misread their own result. Enter
only your salary while living in a two-earner home and you're comparing your
half against everyone else's whole — a mistake that can move your percentile
by tens of points. [Household income vs. individual
income](/us/insights/household-vs-individual-income) works through exactly
how far off that comparison gets, and what to enter instead.

It also means "average salary" and "$80,734" measure different things
entirely: one counts workers, the other counts homes.

## It varies enormously by state

$80,734 is the national figure — but median household income ranges from the
mid-$50,000s to over $100,000 depending on the state. See [our full ranking
of all 50 states](/us/insights/state-median-income-rankings) for the exact
numbers, or jump straight to your state's page for the local breakdown.

And a state median still averages over enormous internal variation — a
county in the same state can sit far above or below its own state's line.
Whether that matters to you personally is really a cost-of-living question,
which [why a high income doesn't always mean you're
ahead](/us/insights/cost-of-living-vs-income) takes apart in detail.

## Why you might have seen a different number

If you've come across a US median household income figure that isn't
$80,734, it isn't necessarily wrong. Three things commonly explain the gap.

**Five-year vs. one-year estimates.** The $80,734 figure comes from the ACS
2020–2024 5-Year Estimates, which pool five years of survey responses. The
Census also publishes 1-year estimates covering a single year, which are
more current but noisier. The two rarely match exactly. Pennsylvania's
5-year median is $77,971 against a 2024 1-year figure of $77,545; Nevada's
are $78,260 and $81,134. Neither version is the "real" one — the 5-year is
more stable and available for small geographies, the 1-year is fresher.
This site uses the 5-year estimates throughout, because they're the only
vintage published down to the county level.

**Age of the householder.** The national median blends every age group
together. Households headed by someone under 25 have a median of $45,332,
while the 45–64 bracket sits at $97,843 — more than double. A "typical"
income depends heavily on which stage of the [earnings
curve](/us/insights/income-by-age-curve) you're looking at.

**A different definition of income.** Some sources report per-capita income,
individual earnings, or family income (which excludes people living alone).
All are legitimate statistics; none of them are household income, and they
produce very different numbers.
